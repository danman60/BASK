Thirteen findings — strongest first
№ 1 · Memberships

Nobody renews a membership they didn’t use

15% → 54% first-month renewal, sorted only by how often they came in

Members who barely used their first month renew one time in seven. Members who used it hard renew better than one in two. That is not a small tilt — it is more than a three-fold swing, and it holds in all four of your salons.

And it has nothing to do with the price. Cheap plans, mid plans, expensive plans — they renew within a few points of each other, with no pattern at all. The member you don’t see is the member you lose, and no discount buys them back.

You can see it coming by mid-month. By the 15th you can pull the one member in four you’ve barely seen — three-quarters of them won’t renew, against two-thirds of everyone else.

3.5× swing across usage price bands: 32.1 · 32.8 · 27.7 · 35.2% — no gradient
Monday: pull every member past day 15 of their month with fewer than four visits. Invite them in — a session reminder, a “your month is half gone” nudge. Not money off. Bask builds that list on the 15th of every month, by itself.
Show me the numbers

First-cycle members whose package ended ≥90 days before the data ends. Renewal = next membership starting ≤90 days after cancel. Renewal by visits during the whole first package: 1–2 → 15.5%; 3–5 → 20.2%; 6–9 → 33.2%; 10–14 → 41.3%; 15+ → 54.2%. Point-biserial r=0.215. Direction holds in all four salons.

Price is genuinely null: ≤$35 → 32.1%; $36–65 → 32.8%; $66–90 → 27.7%; >$90 → 35.2%. No monotone trend in either direction.

Overall first-cycle renewal is 27.8% once left-censoring is removed (people already long-standing before the extract opened were inflating the raw 32.0%).

Four separate attempts to break this failed. (1) Duration proxy — 92% of packages run 28–32 days and the median is exactly 30.0 in every price band, so expensive plans are not simply longer. (2) Cheap-plans-are-different — no. (3) Price masked by usage — no. (4) Left-censoring — de-censoring makes the gradient steeper (8.1% → 52.1%, r=0.233 vs 0.215), not weaker.

Honest limit on the mid-month flag: it catches about 31% of eventual non-renewers at 1.12× lift over the base rate. It is a good place to spend a free text message, not a precision instrument. And low usage partly reveals lost interest rather than causing it — a nudge will not recover the whole gap.

№ 2 · Lotion

The $9 sachet is not a sample. It’s a dead end.

14% vs 55% odds the next lotion is a bottle — sachet-first buyer vs bottle-first buyer

Seven in ten of your lotion customers buy a sachet first. Take the ones who come back and buy lotion a second time — the bottle-first customer moves to another bottle 54.6% of the time. The sachet-first customer moves up 14.1% of the time. Same shelf, same staff, same second purchase. Four times the difference.

They are not sampling their way up. They are settling in — and paying $19.00 an ounce to do it, when the bottle on the same shelf is $4.53 an ounce. Your most frequent lotion buyers are your worst-served lotion buyers.

Nobody graduates without being asked.

sachet $19.00/oz · bottle $4.53/oz 69.6% of lotion customers start on a sachet only 15% ever buy a bottle
Monday: the moment someone reaches for a second sachet, say the sentence — “two of these cost more than half the bottle.” That moment walks up to your register on its own, roughly every other sachet customer. For UVALUX: this is what a sampling programme should be measured on — sachet-to-bottle conversion, per account, per quarter. Right now the sample is the product.
Show me the numbers

First lotion purchase per customer, size parsed from the SKU on 475 of 598 lotion SKUs. 69.6% start at ≤1 oz. Packette median $9.50 = $19.00/oz; bottle (>2 oz) median $38.50 = $4.53/oz. 15.0% of packette-first buyers ever buy a bottle.

The sharper cut, conditional on buying lotion a second time at all: packette-first move to a bottle 14.1% of the time, bottle-first 54.6%. This removes the “they just don’t come back” explanation — both groups came back.

Attacks that failed: the unparsed SKUs are only 2.4% of lotion lines and cannot move the result; only two SKUs fall in the 1–2 oz gap, so the sachet/bottle split hides no middle category; three independent ways of computing $/oz agree (4.19× / 4.19× / 3.92×).

Over a year, packette-first buyers spend mean $29.10 on lotion against bottle-first $57.70. That gap carries selection — bottle-first buyers were more committed to begin with — so the mechanism (nobody is asked to trade up) is the claim, not the full gap.

No margin claim is made anywhere in this finding: unit cost is empty in 100% of these records, so we do not know what any of it costs you.

№ 3 · Memberships

When a month runs out you have fourteen days. Then a cliff.

14 days flat for two weeks — then renew-again odds fall off

A member who buys their next month inside two weeks stays on the member path: better than half of them renew again after that. It makes no difference whether they buy on the day it expired or on day thirteen — the odds are flat across the whole fortnight.

Past day fourteen it drops. Fifteen to thirty days out, renew-again falls to 40%. Past a month, 33%. That is not a gentle decay — it is a plateau and then a step. You have a deadline, and it is fourteen days long.

inside 14 days: ~53% renew again after 30 days: 33%
Monday: put every expired member on a fourteen-day clock, and make the touch land at expiry + 7 — “your month ran out last week, want to keep going?” Month-end is too late; that is the wrong side of the cliff. Bask runs the expiry calendar and counts the days down for you.
Show me the numbers

Second packages, bucketed by how late they started after the first expired. Outcome = a third package within 90 days. Bought early or on time → 51.0%; 1–3 days late → 54.4%; 4–7 → 55.9%; 8–14 → 52.6%; 15–30 → 40.3%; 31–90 → 33.2%.

The plateau is genuinely flat — the four buckets inside 14 days are statistically indistinguishable from one another (χ² p=0.737). The cliff is genuinely real — ≤14 days 53.4% vs 15–90 days 36.3%, p=6.4×10⁻⁹. It survives the de-censoring correction that changed other figures in this report.

Bucket sizes run 147–286. Prompt rebuyers are keener members to begin with, so treat this as a window you should be inside, not a save rate you are promised.

№ 4 · Memberships

If they don’t join on the first two visits, they never join

76.8% of everyone who ever became a member joined on visit one

87% joined by visit two. Everything after that is a rounding error spread across four years.

Here is the part nobody would guess. A regular on their twelfth un-membered visit is no more likely to join than someone on their second. Not slightly less likely — the same, at every single step from one to twelve. Loyalty does not ripen into membership here. The moment is at the beginning or it never happens.

visit 1: 76.8% · by visit 2: 87.2% · by visit 5: 92.3% visit 12 converts no better than visit 2
Monday: move the membership conversation onto visits one and two, and put your best closer there. Then stop re-pitching your long-time regulars — they have already answered. Sell them a different thing: a punch card, a loyalty rate, a bundle. The monthly close is not going to land on the twelfth ask either.
Show me the numbers

New joiners: 76.8% join at visit 1, 87.2% by visit 2, 92.3% by visit 5.

This one is cohort-insensitive — tightening the definition of “new” to exclude anyone who was already a customer before our records open barely moves it (75.0 / 88.1 / 92.7). It does not inherit the cohort correction applied elsewhere in this report.

The flat conditional is the real finding: among customers with at least k visits and no membership yet, the probability of ever joining sits at 8.7–10.1% for every k from 1 to 12. It survives the censoring attack; forcing a full extra year of observation shifts the level but not the flatness.

Pairs with the banked staffing result — day-one conversion ranges 3.5% to 44.3% depending who is working. That staffer effect operates inside exactly this two-visit window.

№ 5 · New customers

A first-timer decides in a week, not a month

61% → 27% odds a first-timer ever comes back — overall, then after seven silent days

Six in ten first-timers come back within six months. But if a week goes by with no second visit, that collapses to fewer than three in ten — and it keeps falling fast: two in ten after fourteen days, one in seven after a month, one in twenty after ninety days.

Which means the month-end follow-up everyone runs is a conversation with people who already decided no. The decision is made in week one. Be there in week one.

~320 undecided first-timers per salon each year a rescued late returner spends $94.08 in year one
Monday: one message on day 5 to every first-timer who hasn’t come back — “we’d love to see you again.” Bask prints that list daily; it is never more than a handful of names. Each percentage point of that pool you convert is worth about $293 a year per salon — sized from what those customers actually went on to spend, not a promise that the text works.
Show me the numbers

Genuinely new customers with at least 180 days of observation, excluding anyone whose account predates the extract (n=6,214): 60.6% return within 180 days.

Conditional survival — not back by day 7 → 27.3% ever return; day 14 → 19.5%; day 28 → 13.5%; day 90 → 4.9%. Monotone, steep, and consistent with the banked activation cliff — this is that cliff’s clock.

Value: a late returner — second visit between day 8 and day 180 — is worth $94.08 in their first 365 days. That figure keeps the zero-spend customers in the denominator, which is why it is $94 and not the $113 you get by quietly dropping them.

Sizing: the undecided-at-day-7 pool is ~322 customers per salon-year; one percentage point of extra conversion is ~3.2 customers ≈ $293 per salon-year. Modest money, and the lever costs a text message. The window is proven; the lift from outreach is not tested in this data and is not claimed.

№ 6 · Winning people back

You cannot feel them leaving. You can only count the days.

$93 vs $63 a name on your win-back list vs an unknown walk-in, next twelve months

Everyone believes customers slow down before they quit. Yours don’t. We took every regular who disappeared and looked at their last three visits: they came in on the same rhythm as the people who stayed. There is no wobble, no warning, no tell. An alert watching for “they’re slowing down” is wrong nine times out of ten.

So the only trigger that works is the calendar — and the calendar is good. You can’t know which walk-in will come back. You can know that a name on your ninety-days-quiet list is worth about $93 over the next year, where an unknown new face averages $63. Same effort, known target, better odds.

One thing to be honest about: this is a cycle. About 62% of the people you win back go quiet again. That is not a failure — it is a reason to run the list every month instead of once.

“they’re slowing down” alerts: 8.7% right a known name beats an unknown one by ~50%
Monday: run the ninety-days-quiet list, every month, forever. And split it by how long they’d been coming — the ones you lost were mostly burst tanners on a run (they were coming in every three days, not every five), so the message that reaches a two-year regular is not the message that reaches them.
Show me the numbers

Lapsed regulars (8+ visits, then 120+ days of silence, n=3,458): final-three-visit gap ratio 1.21 against their own baseline — statistically indistinguishable from still- active regulars’ 1.17 (Mann-Whitney p=0.183). An interval-stretch alert tops out at 8.7% precision, 1.53× lift. Useless as a trigger.

The tenure detail: the regulars who lapsed had a shorter baseline gap than the ones who stayed — 3.0 days vs 5.0 days, p=1.5×10⁻²³. They were burst tanners on a run, not habituated regulars decaying. Same conclusion, better explanation, and it is why the list should be segmented by tenure.

Targeting value: first reactivation events with a full year of follow-on observation (n=3,929) earn $93.44 mean over the next 365 days. New customers average $63. The claim here is targeting, not superiority — see “Things we checked”, where we kill the stronger version of this ourselves.

Re-lapse rate is 62.1%.

№ 7 · Winning people back

The comeback visit closes memberships better than any other visit

28.7% of comeback visits end with a membership sold that same day

Nearly three in ten. That is two and a half times the rate of a comparable ordinary visit — comparing like with like, against customers who could actually have started a membership that day.

The person walking back through your door after months away is not a retention problem. They are the warmest buyer in the building — someone who already decided, on their own, that they wanted to tan again. Treat that visit like a first visit and it converts like one.

2.5× the membership close of a comparable visit
Monday: script the comeback. Greet them by name, walk them round what’s changed, offer the join — the same routine your best closer runs on a first visit. Which means the win-back list from № 6 is not a retention cost. It is the highest-converting sales audience you have, and you can print it.
Show me the numbers

Reactivation visits (a return after a gap of 90 days or more, n=9,741): a membership is purchased that same day 28.7% of the time.

The honest comparison: 54% of all other visits happen inside an active package, where starting a new membership is nearly impossible — comparing against that base flatters the result. Restricted to like-for-like visits where a membership start was actually available, the multiple is 2.5×, not the 4.4× the raw comparison suggests.

The 90-day cut is a convention, not a threshold in the data — the effect strengthens gradually with the length of the gap rather than switching on. A longer-gone customer is a warmer one.

Not claimed: a matching retail effect. Lotion attaches on comeback visits at 13.8% — lower than the 18.6% on first visits. The comeback is a membership moment, not a retail moment, and we are not going to tell you otherwise because it would sound better.

№ 8 · The four salons together

A quarter of your money shops at more than one of your stores

$533,585 25.5% of all revenue, from customers who use more than one location

One customer in nine has been to more than one of your four salons. That group is a quarter of all your revenue and 30% of all your visits. Tighten it right down — count only people with three or more visits at a second store, no accidental drop-ins — and it is still 17% of everything you take.

Your best customers do not experience four businesses. They experience one, and they move around inside it. Anything that makes the four look different to them — a membership that doesn’t travel, a lotion that’s only on one shelf, a price that changes across town — is friction you are charging your heaviest spenders.

11.5% of customers → 25.5% of revenue and 11.5% is a floor, not a ceiling
Monday: make the four stores behave like one for the people who already treat them that way — memberships honoured everywhere, the same lotion on every shelf, the same price on the wall. For UVALUX: stock and sell this group as one market, not four accounts. A gap on one location’s shelf leaks the whole group’s best baskets, and per-store numbers will never show you why.
Show me the numbers

Customers visiting 2+ salons: 1,314 of 11,411 ever-visited (11.5%), carrying 30.1% of visits and $533,585 = 25.5% of revenue. At the strict threshold of 3+ visits at the second salon: 5.7% of customers, 16.9% of revenue. 11.2% of all visits happen away from the customer’s home salon.

The obvious attack — “you’ve just merged two different people’s ID numbers” — fails. The customer ID embeds the salon ID, so a collision between salons is impossible; a shared ID is a genuine upstream link. And the cross-salon share is stable year over year (9.3% → 13.5%), so it is not an artefact of customers migrating between locations.

11.5% is a floor. Anyone holding two separate accounts at two salons is invisible to this count, so the true figure is higher.

What we do not claim: that these customers use two salons heavily. Half of them have two or fewer lifetime visits at the second store. The revenue concentration is the finding; the usage headline is not. Heavy customers roam because they visit a lot — read this as market structure, not cause and effect.

Data note: the salon ID must be read as text. Parsing it as a number silently drops a leading zero and inflates this figure to a nonsense 77%.

№ 9 · The membership annuity

The first renewal is the only hard one

28% → 87% renewal odds, first cycle to fifteenth

Fewer than three members in ten renew their first month. Then it climbs, almost every single cycle: 39%, 47%, 54%, 59% … and by the tenth month nearly eight in ten, on up to 87%.

Every renewal you win compounds — the next one is 20 to 45% more likely than the one before. A member on their tenth month is a different animal from a member on their first, and they got there one save at a time.

Which tells you exactly where to spend: all of the mortality is on the bottom step. Findings № 1, 3 and 4 all operate on that step, and nothing else in this document matters as much.

members: 33.7% of customers, 56.0% of revenue members out-earn casuals by $69/yr
Monday: stop spreading your attention evenly across members. Every save you make in month one is worth several months of a mature member’s attention, because the escalator does the rest of the work for free. Bask can show you the cycle number next to every member’s name — that one column reorders your whole day.
Show me the numbers

Renewal by cycle: 32.0% (1→2), 39.2%, 47.5%, 53.8%, 58.7%, 60.7%, 64.4%, 70.4%, 67.7%, 77.4% (10→11) … 86.6% (15→16). n falls from 3,741 to 119 along the curve.

Two honesty notes. First, that 67.7% is a genuine exception — the climb dips once, from cycle 8 to cycle 9. “Every cycle without exception” would be a lie, and the exception is right there in your own numbers. Second, the cycle-1 figure of 32.0% is itself left-censored; de-censored it is 27.8%, which makes the climb steeper, not shallower.

Compounding: the multiple on expected remaining packages per renewal won is 1.45× / 1.40× / 1.28× / 1.22× / 1.18× / 1.20× — a real 20–45% per step, and nowhere near the “doubles every time” that a quick read of the curve suggests. Expected remaining packages: ~0.6 at cycle 1, ~1.2 at cycle 3, ~1.9 at cycle 5, ~4.2 at cycle 10.

Member-year vs casual-year, 2018 (the one calendar year fully covered): 4,734 active customers; the 1,627 with any membership coverage average $154 (median 12 visits), the 3,107 casuals average $85 (median 3 visits). That $69 is a difference between two groups, not a promise of what happens when you convert someone — casuals who convert were never average casuals.

№ 10 · New customers, in season

Autumn first-timers are your best ones — and you lose most of them at the door

+21% what an autumn first-timer is worth against a spring one — if you keep them

A first-timer who walks in during September to November comes back 57% of the time. One who walks in during spring, 69%. You lose the autumn recruit far more often.

But the ones you keep are better. An autumn first-timer who sticks is worth $108.69 in their first year against a spring one’s $89.80 — about a fifth more. Fewest recruits, hardest to hold, worth the most: that is the definition of where the follow-up should go first.

autumn 57% return vs spring 69% $108.69 vs $89.80 when kept
Monday: point the day-5 message (№ 5) and your best first-visit person at the September–November walk-ins before anyone else. They arrive in exactly the months when new faces are scarcest, and they are the ones worth chasing hardest.
Show me the numbers

New customers with a full year of observation (n=7,813): 180-day return rate by month of first visit — March–May 69.2% (n=3,183) vs September–November 56.9% (n=1,044), z=7.09.

Value, conditional on returning at all — which is what “worth keeping” means: autumn returners earn $108.69 in their first 365 days vs spring returners’ $89.80 (p=0.008). Comparing everyone including the people who never came back makes the two look equal, but only because autumn has a bigger pile of $0 no-shows dragging its average down. That is an averaging artefact, not a fact about customers.

Whether this is the season itself or simply who walks in during the off-season cannot be separated in this data. The targeting implication is the same either way.

№ 11 · When members join

January members stay. July members are borrowing a tan.

1.75× a January joiner’s renewal odds against a July joiner’s, year for year

A member who signs up in January renews at 38.7% — against 27% for every other month of the year. A member who signs up in July renews at barely half the January rate. They came for a fortnight in the sun and they meant it.

And this is specifically January, not “winter”. December and February members look like everybody else. Resolution season is a real thing and it shows up in your renewal book.

January 38.7% vs 27% all other months “winter is better” is false — it’s January only
Monday (well, in December): put your recruitment push where the good members are — the first three weeks of January. And for the July joiner, stop banking on a renewal that four times in five will not come: sell them the bigger upfront package instead. For UVALUX: January sell-in support belongs next to the March inventory ramp.
Show me the numbers

First-package renewal by start month, on the de-censored cohort (n=2,331): January 38.7% (n=168) vs 27.0% for all other months (n=2,163), χ² p=0.0015; July 20.4% (n=216).

Those raw cells divide out to 1.9×, but the January cell and the July cell do not cover the same years — matching year for year, the honest multiple is 1.75×. That is the number we print.

The broad seasonal story is dead: December-January-February 31.1% vs March-April-May 28.2%, p=0.30. Only January survives.

A correction that must travel with this figure: the naive all-data January number is 51.3%, and it is wrong — 58% of that sample starts in January 2016, which is not new joiners but pre-2016 members renewing invisibly. 38.7% is the honest one.

Moderate confidence: n=168 in the clean January cell. Direction repeats in 2017, 2018 and 2019 taken separately (31.8% / 40.9% / 44.0% vs ~28%), but each single year is thin.

№ 12 · The buddy economy

313 pairs of your customers always arrive together

50× how far above chance these pairings are — 313 pairs, 554 named customers

Not “sometimes overlap”. 554 customers who show up in the same minute as the same other person, over and over, for years. We tried hard to make this go away — reshuffling everyone’s visits at random, thousands of times, keeping each person’s exact visit count. Chance produces five such pairs. Your salons have 313.

These are real relationships, and they are sitting in your till log with names attached. You can build a product for them tomorrow.

313 stable pairs · 554 customers what we are NOT claiming: that pairs quit together — see below
Monday: sell the duo. A two-person membership, a bring-your-person week, a referral that names the actual person they already come with. Bask can list every stable pair in your book from the check-in log alone — no survey, no app, no asking anyone anything.
Show me the numbers

Co-arrival = two different customers checking in at the same salon within 180 seconds. 313 pairs co-arrived 5+ times, containing 554 customers.

The kill attempt that failed: permutation nulls that preserve every customer’s exact visit count produce 4.5–6.7 such pairs — roughly 50× below the observed 313, p<0.001. Repeat co-arrival is a genuine signal, not a scheduling coincidence.

Sensitivity, disclosed: at a stricter 60-second window the count falls to 212 pairs (−32%). The pairs are real at either threshold; the exact count depends on where you draw the line.

Two claims we removed rather than show you: that these customers are worth more (they have more visits by construction — controlling for visit volume they are worth less per visit), and that a fifth of your visits arrive as pairs (the co-arrival rate is below what chance alone produces). See “Things we checked”.

№ 13 · The register

Renewal day is the quietest register in the shop

560 renewal moments per salon per year — every one of them on your calendar in advance

The day a member hands you money for another month is the day you are least likely to sell them anything else. Renewal transactions attach a retail item 7.7% of the time against 11.7% on other service transactions — a 26% shortfall that survives controlling for veteran customers, who buy less anyway.

Compare that to a signup day, where nearly one transaction in five goes out with lotion. Same commitment, same counter — the difference is that a signup gets a conversation and a renewal gets a receipt.

The dollars here are small and we will say so. What is not small is that these are 560 moments a year you know about in advance. There is no cheaper prompt in the building.

renewal day 7.7% · other days 11.7% · signup day 19.2% worth a few hundred dollars a salon-year — not a windfall
Monday: one line at the register on every renewal — “want the bottle on the package?” Bask can fire the prompt automatically, because the renewal date was already sitting in the system. Do not budget for this one; just stop leaving it on the floor.
Show me the numbers

Attach measured per transaction here (stated because everywhere else in this report attach is per visit). Membership signup-day transactions attach retail 19.2% (n=4,044). Renewal-day 7.7% (n=9,395) vs 11.7% on other service-carrying transactions (n=31,885), χ² p=1.48×10⁻²⁸.

Veteran control — restricted to customers with 10+ lifetime visits, removing the long-customers-buy-less confound: renewal-day 7.6% (n=9,273) vs the same customers’ other service transactions 10.3% (n=23,442), χ² p=6.1×10⁻¹⁴. A 26% relative gap that survives the control.

Volume: 9,395 renewal-day transactions across four salons over the window = about 560 per salon per year. Closing the attach gap at the median attached line (~$9.75) is worth a few hundred dollars per salon per year. This is a systems insight, not a revenue projection, and it should never be presented as one.

The other half of the work

Things we checked so you don’t have to

Every idea below sounds true. Several of them were ours — findings from our own first pass that we then set out to destroy, and did. Knowing what doesn’t hold is how you avoid spending Monday on it, and it is the only reason to trust the thirteen that did.

“A won-back customer is worth more than a new one.”

We wrote that. Then we checked it, and it fell apart. A reactivated customer earns about $93 over the next year — but a new customer who came back even once earns $92, and one with eight visits earns $158. The “premium” was arithmetic: winning someone back requires two visits by definition, while the new-customer average is dragged down by everyone who never returned. Worse, the same reactivated customers earned $108 in the year before they lapsed — more than the $93 after. Winning someone back is partial recovery of a customer you already had. It is not an upgrade on a new one.

What survives is the targeting argument — finding № 6

“Lose one, lose two — when half a pair quits, the other follows.”

Also ours, also dead. Survivors did drop 47% after their partner stopped coming — which looked damning until we asked what a comparable customer does over the same stretch of calendar with no partner leaving at all. They drop 49.5%. The survivors declined less than chance. There is no contagion here; there is a population of customers that fades, and pairs fade with it. The pairs themselves are real (№ 12). The domino effect is not, and we are not going to sell you a flag built on it.

Sell the pairs. Don’t sell the contagion.

“Customers slow down before they quit.”

No. Departing regulars’ final visits are indistinguishable from their normal ones. An alert watching for stretching intervals is right 8.7% of the time. That is precisely why № 6 runs on a calendar instead of on instinct.

“Harvest new customers fast — the value is up front.”

No. A customer pays like an annuity: only 12.5% of a two-year customer’s revenue arrives in the first 30 days, 26% by day 90, 62.6% by day 365. Every finding here is a retention calendar, and that is not a style choice — it is what the money does.

“Cheaper packages renew better.”

No. From your $30 plans to your $140 plans, renewal moves within a few points and in no particular direction. Four separate attempts to find a hidden price effect all failed. This is why not one recommendation in this document is a discount.

“A fifth of your visits walk in as pairs.”

Our own number, and it doesn’t stand. Once you account for how busy your salons are, the rate of two people arriving within three minutes of each other is below what random chance produces. Busy shops make coincidental neighbours. The finding lives in the repeat pairings (№ 12), not the headline count.

“Your beds run nearly empty, so every extra visit is pure profit.”

We could not check this and we will not repeat it. Your register files carry no bed, no room, no capacity and no cost data — there is nothing in them that could produce a utilisation number. If someone shows you one, ask which column it came from.

“A friend’s introduction makes a sticky customer.”

No. First-timers brought in by an existing customer return at 66.3%; solo walk-ins at 63.9%. That difference is noise. The social effect that matters is between established regulars, not at the introduction. And two first-timers arriving together actually stick around less than one arriving alone — those are event tans.

“The faster a first-timer comes back, the better the customer.”

No. Once someone returns at all, how quickly they did it tells you nothing about what they will spend. Week one decides whether (№ 5) — not how much.

“Seeing the same staff member builds loyalty.”

No measurable effect at all. Customers who always saw the same person came back no more often than customers who saw somebody different every time. Which staffer is on the floor for the first visit matters enormously (№ 4). Continuity after that doesn’t.

“Members sneak free tans between packages.”

The opposite. Only 5.8% of a multi-cycle member’s visits fall in an uncovered gap, and 41% of those carry a paid transaction averaging $35. Gap visits are some of the best-paying sessions you sell. Nothing to plug.

“Gift certificates bring in new customers.”

Thirteen gift-certificate payments in four years. Twenty-five coupon redemptions. There is no channel here — which is worth knowing before someone proposes building one.

“First-timers turn up on the weak morning shift.”

No. Only 3.3% of first visits happen before 11am. There is no schedule to fix.

“A crowded floor kills the lotion sale.”

Slightly true, honestly tiny. Attach does slide from 6.5% when someone’s alone to 5.2% in your busiest hours — and fixing all of it is worth under $1,000 a year across all four salons. Keep it as a management observation. Never monetise it.

“Customers with a fixed routine hour are the loyal ones.”

If anything the reverse — the rigid-schedule customers proved slightly more fragile. Flexibility, not routine, marks the ones who stay.

“Letting members downshift to a cheaper plan keeps them longer.”

Probably, but not provably — so it is not a finding. Downgraders did buy more total months than plan-stayers, but the result sits just the wrong side of significance and about a quarter of the “downgrades” are really a $0 package or the same plan renamed. We would rather leave a plausible idea out than put a soft one in front of you.
