Bask What we found in your registers · August 2026
Four salons · four years · every visit, every sale

Your registers already knew. Here is what they said.

We read every visit and every sale across your four salons — a little over 194,000 visits from more than 11,000 customers. What came out is a picture of the business you could not have seen from behind the counter: how it actually makes its money, twelve specific findings, and the thing to do about each one on Monday morning.

A note on honesty: none of these are get-rich numbers. They are percentages that move — a lift on the line each one touches, computed from your own records, nothing invented. Every figure can be opened up — tap "Show me the numbers" under any card. And at the end is the list of things we tested that didn't hold, so nobody sells you those.

First, the shape of the business

Four facts that change what every other number means

Everything else in this report hangs off these.

Your beds sit empty 9 hours in 10

Even at your busiest hour, the floor runs around one-eighth full. So every extra visit, renewal or comeback costs you almost nothing to serve — it lands almost entirely as profit. The whole game is demand, not cost.

Membership is an escalator

Only about 3 in 10 members renew their first month. But every renewal after that gets easier — by the tenth, nearly 8 in 10 renew, and it keeps climbing. Each early save roughly doubles what a member goes on to be worth. All the loss is at the bottom step.

A member-year is worth almost double a casual-year

In a full year, the average member spends about $154 and comes in 12 times; the average casual spends about $85 and comes in 3. On empty beds, each conversion is roughly $70 a year of nearly pure margin.

Tanning is social

A fifth of all visits walk through the door as a pair — two people, same minute. Your customers don't come alone, and (finding № 6) they don't leave alone either.

The whole playbook is five calendars and zero discounts: day-5 for first-timers, day-15 for quiet members, two weeks after every expiry, ninety-days-quiet for comebacks, and a flag when one half of a pair goes missing. Every one of them is a list your own till can print.
Twelve findings — ranked by what they're worth and how fast you can act
№ 1 · New customers

A first-timer decides in days — not weeks

Of the first-timers who come back, three out of four are back within seven days — most within two. If a week passes with no second visit, the odds they ever return drop to about 3 in 10; after a month, about 1 in 6. Waiting until month-end to follow up means talking to people who already decided no.

≈ 320 undecided first-timers per salon each year a rescued first-timer spends ≈ $113 in year one
Monday: send one message — "we'd love to see you again" — to every first-timer who hasn't returned by day 5. Bask builds that list for you daily. It costs a text.
Show me the numbers

True-new customers (first visit ≥ 2016-04-01) with ≥180 days of observation: n=8,732; 64.2% return within 180 days. Among returners, median gap to second visit = 2 days (p25 1, p75 7, p90 31); 76.3% of eventual returners are back within 7 days, 84.4% within 14.

Conditional: not back by day 7 → P(return ≤180d) = 29.8% (n=4,451 at risk); day 14 → 21.9%; day 28 → 15.7%; day 90 → 6.0%. Builds on the banked 40% activation cliff — this is its clock.

Sizing: undecided-at-day-7 pool ≈ 322/salon-yr. Late returners (second visit day 8–180, n=1,327) average $113 first-365-day revenue. Each +1pt of conversion ≈ $360/salon-yr. The lift from outreach itself is untested in this data — the window is what's proven.

№ 2 · Winning people back

The comeback visit is the best sales moment in the shop

The day a lapsed customer walks back in, they join a membership four times as often as on an ordinary visit, and they buy lotion at more than double the usual rate — nearly as often as a brand-new first-timer. A comeback is a second first visit, and it closes better than the first one. They're also worth more afterward: a returned lapser typically spends around $50 over the next year, against a new walk-in's near-nothing — because most walk-ins vanish. And you cannot spot them coming: people here don't slow down before they stop — their last visits look exactly like their normal visits. Only the calendar finds them.

membership close: ~29% on comeback day vs ~7% normally lotion: 2.4× the usual rate
Monday: run the 90-days-quiet list monthly and invite them back — then treat every comeback like a first visit: greet, re-tour, offer the join, offer the bottle. That visit is the hottest buyer in the building.
Show me the numbers

Reactivation visits (return after ≥90d gap): n=9,741. Membership purchased same day: 28.7% vs 6.5% same-day base across all visits (4.4×). Per-visit retail attach on the comeback: 13.74% vs 5.74% all-visit rate (2.4×; first-visit banked rate 16.06%).

Winback value: first reactivation events with ≥365d observation (n=3,929): forward-365 revenue mean $93.40, median $50.00, 12.2 visits — vs true-new customers (n=7,813) mean $62.80, median $4.00.

No-warning result: lapsed regulars (≥8 visits then ≥120d silence, n=3,458) final-3-visit median gap 4.0d vs own baseline 3.0d (ratio 1.20) — indistinguishable from active regulars' 1.17 (Mann-Whitney p=0.18). A gap≥3×-personal-median alert scores 10.6% precision vs 6.9% base rate. Calendar, not vibes.

Caveats: comeback visits are self-selected moments of renewed intent — which is exactly why they're worth staffing for; reactivated lapsers are proven tanners, so this ranks winback targeting above cold acquisition rather than proving the message causes the return.

№ 3 · Memberships

You can tell by the 15th which members won't renew — it's the ones you haven't seen

Members who barely used their first month renew about 1 time in 6. Members who came in a lot renew about 1 in 2. You can see it by mid-month: fewer than four visits by day 15 is the warning light. And it has nothing to do with the package price — cheap and expensive plans renew at the same rate. The member you don't see is the member you lose.

renewal runs ~16% → ~52% with usage
Monday: pull the members past day 15 of their month with fewer than 4 visits, and invite them in — a session reminder, not a discount. Discounts don't move renewal here; showing up does.
Show me the numbers

First-cycle members with package end ≥90d before data end: n=3,741; 32.0% renew (next membership ≤90d after cancel). Renewal by full-cycle visits: 1–2 → 15.6% (n=231); 3–5 → 20.7%; 6–9 → 33.7%; 10–14 → 41.7%; 15+ → 52.3% (n=214). Point-biserial r=0.208, p=6.7e-38; direction holds in all four salons.

First-15-days-only version: 0–1 visits → 17.4% (n=155); 2–3 → 25.3%; 4–5 → 31.7%; 6–7 → 37.2%; 8+ → 42.4% (n=446).

Price null: ≤$35 → 32.5%, $36–65 → 32.8%, $66–90 → 27.7%, >$90 → 35.2%. No gradient.

Caveat: low usage partly reveals already-lost interest, so a nudge won't capture the whole gap — but the gradient is steep and the list is free.

№ 4 · Memberships

When a membership runs out, you have two weeks

A member who buys their next month within two weeks of the old one ending stays on the member path — better than half renew again after that. Let it drift past two weeks and they slide toward occasional: the next renewal falls to about 4 in 10, and past a month, 1 in 3. The window is flat for fourteen days, then it drops.

renew-again odds: ~53% inside 14 days → 33% after a month
Monday: put every expired member on a 14-day clock. One touch at expiry + 7 days — "your month ended last week, want to keep going?" — lands inside the window instead of after it.
Show me the numbers

Cycle-2 packages by start latency after cycle-1 expiry; outcome = cycle-3 renewal ≤90d (observable n=1,188): ≤0d → 51.0% (n=147); 1–3d → 54.4% (n=195); 4–7d → 55.9% (n=188); 8–14d → 52.6% (n=156); 15–30d → 40.3% (n=216); 31–90d → 33.2% (n=286).

Plateau-then-cliff shape (not smooth decay) argues for a real window over pure self-selection, but prompt rebuyers are keener members — present as a window, never a guaranteed save rate. Buckets n=147–286.

№ 5 · Memberships

If they didn't join in the first two visits, they almost never join

Nearly 9 out of 10 of the people who ever became members joined during their first two visits. And here is the part nobody would guess: a regular on their twelfth visit without a membership is no more likely to join than someone on their second. Loyalty doesn't ripen into membership — the moment is at the start, or it isn't.

87% of all joiners join by visit 2
Monday: make the membership conversation a first-two-visits conversation, with your best closer on it. Stop re-pitching the long-time regulars — offer them something different (a punch card, a loyalty rate), because the monthly pitch has already had its answer.
Show me the numbers

True-new joiners n=2,854: 76.8% join at visit 1, 87.2% by visit 2, 92.3% by visit 5.

Conditional flatness is the finding: among customers with ≥k visits and no membership, P(ever join) sits at 8.7–10.1% for every k from 1 through 12.

Pairs with the banked staffer finding (day-1 conversion 3.5%–44.3% by staffer): the staffer effect operates inside exactly this two-visit window.

№ 6 · The buddy economy

Your best customers come in pairs — and when one quits, the other follows

A fifth of all visits arrive as a pair — two people, same minute. About 1 customer in 20 belongs to a steady tanning pair, and that small group carries an eighth of all your revenue, visiting four times more than everyone else. The dangerous part: pairs quit together. When one half stops coming, the other's visits drop by nearly half within three months — and their last days at the salon land within a month of each other three times more often than chance. One more surprise: two first-timers trying it together stick around less than a solo first-timer — friend-group debuts are event tans, not new habits.

5% of customers, 12% of revenue partner leaves → survivor's visits −45% in 90 days
Monday: when a regular goes quiet, check who they always came with — that person just became your highest-risk customer. Win back the pair ("bring her back — two-for-one week"), and offer your steady duos a two-person deal before anyone drifts. For pairs of brand-new faces: sell the occasion, then chase both with the day-5 message.
Show me the numbers

Co-arrival = two different customers checking in at the same salon within 180 seconds: 40,014 events = 20.6% of visits. 35,111 distinct pairs; 313 pairs with ≥5 co-arrivals ("stable"), containing 554 customers (4.9% of ever-visited) averaging 67.3 visits / $451 vs 14.5 / $169 for others; 12.0% of all revenue.

Contagion: among stable pairs both active into 2019 (n=188), partners' final visits land ≤30d apart 46.3% vs 14.9% for random same-salon pairs both active into 2019 (n=11,997) — 3.1×. Direction: pairs splitting ≥120d apart (n=118 survivors with non-zero pre-rate) — survivor's visits 13.5 → 7.4 in the 90d before/after the partner's last visit (−45.4%, Wilcoxon p=3.2e-11; 80.5% declined). Common shocks can't be fully excluded; for targeting it doesn't matter — the partner's lapse is observable and predictive either way.

First-visit company: brought-by-existing-customer 66.3% return vs solo 63.9% (p=0.036, trivial); two first-timers together 56.9% vs solo 63.9% (χ² p=0.0018, n=522).

№ 7 · Lotion

The $9 sachet isn't a sample — it's where your lotion customers get stuck

7 in 10 customers who ever buy lotion start with a small sachet — and only 1 in 7 of them ever moves up to a bottle. They're not sampling their way to the bottle; they're settling in. They even come back for more sachets — paying roughly four times the price per ounce — while a bottle-first customer spends about twice as much on lotion over the year. Nobody graduates without being asked.

sachet ≈ $19/oz · bottle ≈ $4.50/oz only 15% of sachet-first buyers ever buy a bottle
Monday: the moment someone buys their second sachet, say the sentence: "two of these costs more than half the bottle." Half of sachet buyers come back to buy again — the trade-up moment walks up to the register on its own. For UVALUX: this is what a sampling program should be measured on — sachet-to-bottle conversion, per account.
Show me the numbers

First lotion purchase per buyer, size parseable on 472/598 lotion SKUs (buyer n=3,271): 69.6% start ≤1 oz. Packette median $9.50 = $19.00/oz; bottle (>2 oz) median $38.50 = $4.53/oz.

14.9% of packette-first buyers ever buy a bottle (vs 26.9% of bottle-first buying another). Packette-first repurchase anything more often (50.1% vs 38.1%) — loyal to the sachet. Median time to bottle among the 340 who graduated: 67.5 days.

365-day lotion spend (≥365d obs): packette-first mean $29.10 / median $11.20 (n=1,939) vs bottle-first mean $57.70 / median $43.00 (n=898), Mann-Whitney p=2.0e-168. The spend gap carries selection — sell the mechanism, not the full gap.

№ 8 · The four salons together

A quarter of your money walks between your stores

About one customer in nine uses more than one of the four salons — and that small group carries a quarter of all the revenue and nearly a third of all the visits. Your very best customers don't think of your locations as separate businesses. They think of you as one.

11.5% of customers → 25.5% of revenue ($533k over the window)
Monday: make the four stores feel like one to the people who already treat them that way — memberships honored everywhere, same lotion on the shelf, same prices. For UVALUX: stock and sell this group as one market — a gap in one store's assortment leaks the whole group's best baskets.
Show me the numbers

Customers visiting ≥2 salons: 1,314 of 11,411 ever-visited (11.5%), carrying 30.1% of visits and $533,585 = 25.5% of revenue. Multi-salon average 44.5 visits / $406 vs 13.5 / $154 single-salon (visit-count MWU p=6.4e-255). 11.2% of all visits happen away from the home salon (home = customers.salon_id).

Heaviness→roaming is selection (heavy users roam because they visit a lot) — this is market structure, not causation. Data note: salon_id must be parsed as a string; integer parsing drops a leading zero and inflates this figure to a false 77%.

№ 9 · Memberships

Let them downshift — a cheaper plan keeps them longer

A member who drops to a cheaper plan isn't halfway out the door. The members who downgraded after their first month went on to buy more total months than the ones who never changed plans. The downgrade is a retention move wearing a churn costume — and since price doesn't drive renewal anyway (№ 3), blocking it only loses the whole relationship.

downgraders average ~6 packages · non-changers ~5
Monday: when a member hesitates at renewal, the save offer is the cheaper tier — never a discount on the same tier. Make the downshift easy and visible. A smaller monthly beats no monthly, and the escalator does the rest.
Show me the numbers

Cycle-to-cycle plan moves with both prices known (n=9,374): 73.9% same, 15.1% up (>+10%), 11.0% down (>−10%). Cycle-2 movers: downgraders (n=254) average 6.06 total packages (median 4) vs same-price 5.23 (median 3, n=1,529) vs upgraders 5.42 (n=366). Lifetime revenue $415 / $427 / $474 — the downgrader trades a little revenue for the most tenure; the upgrader is worth most of all.

Moderate confidence: n=254 in the key cell; direction consistent, magnitudes modest. Claim is "downgrades are safe", not "downgrades are better".

№ 10 · When members join

January members stay. July members are borrowing a tan.

A member who signs up in January renews at nearly 4 in 10. One who signs up in July renews at 2 in 10 — they're here for a season, not a habit. The January joiner is the best member you recruit all year, and almost twice the member a midsummer one is.

January renewal ≈ 39% · July ≈ 20%
Monday (for January): put your recruitment push where the good members are — resolution season. For summer joiners: sell the bigger upfront package instead of banking on a renewal that four times out of five won't come.
Show me the numbers

First-package renewal by start month, de-censored (first package ≥2016-07-01 AND first visit ≥2016-04-01, n=2,331): January 38.7% (n=168) vs 27.0% all other months (n=2,163), χ² p=0.0015; July 20.4% (n=216).

Broad winter-vs-spring is NOT significant (DJF 31.1% vs MAM 28.2%, p=0.30) — the effect is January-specific. Direction holds in 2017/2018/2019 separately (31.8%/40.9%/44.0% vs ~28%) but each year is thin.

Correction that must travel: the naive all-data January figure is 51.3% — inflated by left-censoring (Jan-2016 "first" packages are largely pre-2016 members renewing). 38.7% is the honest number. Moderate confidence: n=168 in the clean January cell.

№ 11 · New customers, in season

Autumn first-timers vanish more — and they're worth just as much

A first-timer who walks in between September and November comes back 57% of the time; one who walks in during spring, 69%. But when an autumn first-timer does stay, they're worth exactly as much as anyone else. You lose them at the door, not in the wallet — in the exact months when every new face is scarcest.

57% autumn return vs 69% spring — same value when kept
Monday: aim the day-5 follow-up (№ 1) and your best first-visit people at the September–November walk-ins first. Fewest recruits, hardest to keep, worth the same — that's where the follow-up pays most.
Show me the numbers

True-new with ≥365d observation (n=7,813): 180-day return by first-visit month — Mar–May 69.2% (n=3,183) vs Sep–Nov 56.9% (n=1,044), z=7.09. First-365-day revenue essentially identical (mean $62.80 vs $63.10).

Mechanism (the season itself vs who walks in off-season) is not separable in this data; the targeting implication holds either way.

№ 12 · Lotion

Renewal day is the quietest register in the shop

The day a member hands you money for another month is the day you're least likely to sell them anything else — lotion goes home with them about half as often as on a signup day. Yet these are the easiest moments in your whole calendar: you know they're coming, roughly 2,700 of them a year per salon, and the customer has just re-committed to a month of tanning. That's the person who needs the bottle.

small dollars, big habit — worth fixing because it's free
Monday: one line at the register on every renewal: "want the bottle on the package?" Bask can prompt it automatically — the renewal is already on the calendar.
Show me the numbers

Per-TRANSACTION attach (retail_revenue > 0 — stated because the banked convention is per-visit): signup-day transactions 19.2% (n=4,044); renewal-day 7.7% (n=9,395) vs 11.7% other service-carrying transactions (n=31,885), χ² p=9.1e-41.

Veteran control (≥10 lifetime visits): renewal-day 7.6% (n=9,273) vs same customers' other service transactions 10.3% (n=23,442), χ² p=6.1e-14 — a 26% relative gap that survives the visit-rank decay confound.

Honest sizing: closing the gap at the median attached line (~$9.75) is worth only a few hundred dollars a year per salon. The value is the trigger: scheduled, known-in-advance moments. Never present this one as a revenue projection.

The other half of the work

Things we checked so you don't have to

Every idea below sounds true. We tested each one against your records, and the records said no. Knowing what doesn't work is how you avoid spending Monday on it.

"Customers slow down before they quit."

No. Departing regulars' last visits look exactly like their normal visits — an alert watching for "slowing down" was wrong 9 times in 10. That's why the comeback list (№ 2) and the pair flag (№ 6) run on the calendar, not on gut feel.

"Harvest new customers fast — their value is up front."

No. A customer pays you like an annuity: only an eighth of their two-year value arrives in the first month, a quarter by day 90. The money is in keeping them, which is why every finding here is a retention calendar and none is a squeeze.

"A friend's introduction makes a sticky customer."

Barely. First-timers brought by an existing customer return at almost the same rate as solo walk-ins. The social effect that matters is between established regulars (№ 6) — and two first-timers arriving together actually stick less.

"The faster a first-timer comes back, the better the customer."

No. Once someone returns at all, how quickly tells you nothing about what they'll spend. The week-one window (№ 1) matters for whether they return — not for what they're worth after.

"Seeing the same staff member builds loyalty."

No measurable effect. Customers who always saw the same person came back no more than customers who saw somebody different every time.

"Cheaper packages renew better."

No. Renewal is the same from the $30 plan to the $140 plan. Usage decides (№ 3), not price — which is also why discounting is the wrong save, and why the downshift (№ 9) is safe.

"Members sneak free tans between packages."

The opposite. The few visits members make between packages are paid sessions at nearly three times the usual register rate. No leakage to plug.

"Gift certificates bring in new customers."

Thirteen gift-certificate payments in four years. There is no gift channel to build on yet — which itself is worth knowing before anyone proposes one.

"First-timers arrive on the weak morning shift."

No — almost no first visits happen before 11am, so there's no schedule to fix there.

"A crowded floor kills the lotion sale."

Slightly true, honestly tiny. Selling does dip in the busiest hours, but fixing all of it is worth under $1,000 a year across all four salons. Not worth a Monday.

"Winter joiners are better members across the board."

No — it's January specifically (№ 10). December and February members look like everyone else.

"Customers with a fixed routine hour are the loyal ones."

If anything the reverse — the rigid-schedule customers proved slightly more fragile. Flexibility, not routine, marks the stayers.
